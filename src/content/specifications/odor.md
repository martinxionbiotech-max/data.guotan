  Odor describes what a charcoal releases to the senses during lighting and the
  early phase of combustion — a combination of tars, hydrocarbons and other
  volatiles driven off as the fuel heats. For hookah/shisha use the buyer's
  question is practical: does the charcoal add any taste or smell of its own to
  the session, or does it burn cleanly and neutrally as a heat source?
why_it_matters: >-
  For hookah charcoal, odor is one of the experience metrics that decides
  acceptance, because a clean session is the product. A charcoal that smells
  chemical, smoky or off during lighting transfers that impression straight into
  the customer's perception of the session. Unlike ash or moisture, odor is not
  something a chemical datasheet can directly report — which is exactly why it
  is tested on a sample, in the buyer's own setup, rather than accepted from a
  specification.
typical_method: >-
  There is no single universal laboratory standard for charcoal odor, because
  odor is a sensory property rather than a chemical fraction. In practice it is
  assessed by controlled burn testing: a defined quantity of charcoal is lit on
  a defined heat source and evaluated by a panel during lighting and the first
  minutes of combustion, sometimes with the aid of descriptive sensory scales.
  The nearest laboratory proxy is volatile matter, the fraction released as gas
  or vapour on heating (ASTM D1762-style proximate analysis for wood charcoal,
  or ISO 18123 for solid biofuels), which correlates with start-up smoke and
  odor but is not the same thing as what a user actually smells.
buyer_relevance: >-
  Odor is best treated as a sample-test decision, not a specification line. A
  buyer should assess it blind in their own bowl and burner, and should ask the
  supplier the two questions that predict it: whether the feedstock is
  single-source coconut shell or blended, and how completely the material was
  carbonised. A low volatile-matter figure on the same batch is a supporting
  indicator, but the deciding evidence is a blind session test.
limitations: >-
  Odor is subjective and system-dependent: the same charcoal can read
  differently on different burners, bowls and draw patterns, and panel
  assessments vary between people and sessions. Volatile matter is a fraction,
  not a smell, and does not capture the behaviour of binders or additives that
  also release on heating. No single number substitutes for a sample burned in
  the buyer's own setup, so reported odor quality should always be re-verified
  on a sample.

## How odor relates to the other metrics

Odor is the sensory face of [volatile matter](/specifications/volatile-matter/),
which is released when the fuel is heated. A more thoroughly carbonised, single-
source product retains less volatile material and generally burns cleaner. Read
odor together with [moisture](/specifications/moisture/), since a damper piece
lights less cleanly and releases more start-up steam and smell.

## What a buyer should ask for

- Whether the feedstock is single-source coconut shell or blended.
- The volatile-matter figure and the method used to obtain it.
- The moisture figure, and how packaging controls it in transit.
- A sample batch for a blind session test in the buyer's own hardware.
